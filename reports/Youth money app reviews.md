# Win young savers by never trapping money

Across more than a dozen youth and young-adult money apps, reviews tell the same story. **People love automation that saves for them without willpower, real-time visibility into their money, and cheap access to real investing and credit building. They hate the moment money gets stuck.** That means frozen or closed accounts, slow withdrawals and refunds, flat monthly fees that eat small balances, cancellation traps, and support that never answers. The most-wanted missing features are mostly the opposite of those failures: free use at small balances, real interest on savings, plain-English credit help, an easy way out, and more independence for teens. Lessons on their own are rarely why anyone picks or stays with an app. Parents and teens credit "learning by doing" (earning, saving, investing real money), and the best-known learn-and-earn app, Zogo, draws its angriest reviews from cutting its rewards. For Sprout, this means three things. **Build** around pay-yourself-first automation, named savings goals, credit literacy and real-action milestones. **Avoid** holding customer money in v1, flat fees, upsells inside lessons, quiz-farming rewards and any nudge toward risky trading. **Position** as the free, no-catch, never-stuck app that teaches by doing and grows with the student from high school through college. One important caveat: the network blocked direct page loads of app stores, Trustpilot, BBB and most review sites. **Nearly every rating and review count below comes from search-engine snippets, not live pages.** Treat the numbers as approximate, and check them before quoting them to investors or press.

## Four loved features recur across every category

The most consistent praise is for **automation that takes the decision away from the user**. Reddit users credit Acorns Round-Ups with making investing effortless ([joingerald, secondary summary of Reddit](https://joingerald.com/learn/saving--investing/acorns-reddit-worth-it-2026)). Qapital's draw is "set it and forget it" rules: more than 15 rule types, including a "Guilty Pleasure" rule that saves money every time you shop somewhere you want to cut back ([Savology](https://savology.com/qapital-review); [Money Crashers](https://www.moneycrashers.com/qapital-review/)). One Chime reviewer built an emergency fund in six months simply by letting the app move **10% of each paycheck** into savings ([Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/money/chime-review)). That is a pay-yourself-first plan in all but name. Parent-run kids' apps get the same praise in a different form: chores that pay out automatically and allowances that arrive on schedule are the core reason families use Greenlight, Acorns Early and BusyKid ([Trustpilot Greenlight](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/greenlight.com); [Finder BusyKid](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/busykid-prepaid-card)).

Second is **seeing your money in real time**. Greenlight parents value an alert every time the card is used and the ability to choose where it works ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/software-services/greenlight-review)). Rocket Money's hook is finding forgotten subscriptions ([CNBC Select](https://www.cnbc.com/select/rocket-money-review/)). Among Gen Z, 53% say tracking spending is one of the app features they use most ([Academy Bank](https://www.academybank.com/learn/media/in-the-news/academy-bank-survey-reveals-insights-into-gen-z-financial-habits-credit-knowledge-and-challenges)).

Third is **cheap access to real investing**: zero commissions, fractional shares (buying a slice of a stock for as little as $1) and no minimums. Fidelity Youth gives 13–17-year-olds their own fee-free account with fractional shares and a debit card ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/go/youth-account/overview)). Finder calls BusyKid's access to 4,000+ real stocks for about $48 a year "rare among kids' cards" ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/busykid-prepaid-card)). These features are now standard everywhere, though, so they no longer set any one app apart ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/fidelity-vs-robinhood)).

Fourth is **credit building for young people**. Step's best-loved feature is that it is described as the only secured credit card available to under-18s, with no fees and reporting to all three credit bureaus ([Young and the Invested](https://youngandtheinvested.com/step-banking-card-review/)).

Two smaller themes matter for Sprout's design. Users credit **a small jolt at the moment of spending** with changing habits: one Cleo user said the app's "roast" feature "actually makes me think before I spend $40 on DoorDash again" ([cashadvanceapps.com user reviews](https://www.cashadvanceapps.com/user-reviews/cleo-user-reviews/)). And Zogo users praise **real rewards for learning**: short lessons that "legitimately" pay out gift cards ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1474636588/zogo-finance-simplified/reviews)). As the next section shows, that praise is fragile.

Formal lessons sit lower than any of these. Greenlight's Level Up curriculum and Acorns Early's 100+ "Money Missions" are heavily marketed, and parents speak well of them. But the Trustpilot summaries that mention "teaching financial responsibility" give the credit to the real-money experience (chores, saving, investing), not to lesson modules ([Trustpilot Greenlight](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/greenlight.com); [Trustpilot BusyKid](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/busykid.com)). The research found **no independent usage or completion data** for any of these lesson products.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Automatic saving: round-ups, rules, paycheck splits, auto-paid chores and allowance | Acorns, Qapital, Chime, Greenlight, Acorns Early, BusyKid |
| 2 | Real-time visibility and control over spending | Greenlight, Rocket Money, Gen Z surveys |
| 3 | Free or cheap real investing (fractional shares, $0 commissions) | Fidelity Youth, BusyKid, Robinhood, Public, Webull |

## Stuck money is the single biggest source of anger

The ranking of complaints is clearer than the ranking of praise, because the same failures repeat in app after app and in government enforcement actions.

**Number one is money that users cannot reach.** Step reviewers describe accounts "limited as part of Step's safety review," two months unable to withdraw or spend, and closure emails that give no specific reason ([Trustpilot Step](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/step.com); [MyBankTracker](https://www.mybanktracker.com/checking/reviews/step-card-teen-banking-account-review-432126)). On Robinhood, users describe unexplained restrictions and an account locked after a $4,900 withdrawal request ([Trustpilot Robinhood](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/robinhood.com)). One aggregator estimates that about 30% of Cash App's 1-star reviews concern accounts closed with no reason given ([unstar.app, lower-reliability aggregator](https://unstar.app/blog/is-cash-app-legit-safe-scam-reviews-2026)). Kids' apps show the same pattern on a smaller scale. Greenlight parents report difficulty closing accounts and recovering funds ([FinanceBuzz](https://financebuzz.com/greenlight-review)). An Acorns Early parent waited over a month for a refund after cancelling ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/gohenry-kids-debit-card-app)). A BusyKid customer filed a BBB complaint after cancelling in March 2026 and getting no refund of about $230 ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/us/az/scottsdale/profile/prepaid-credit-cards/busykid-1126-1000076301/complaints)). The researchers' read is that app-store stars come from happy everyday users responding to in-app prompts, while complaint sites fill up with people whose money is stuck. That explains why almost every app here is rated about 4.5–4.8 in the stores and far lower on Trustpilot and BBB.

**Number two is flat fees that are large next to a young person's balance.** Acorns' $3 a month equals 3.6% a year on a $1,000 balance ([Financer](https://financer.com/review/acorns/)). On a $300 balance, a $36 annual fee is about 12%, and Reddit users describe leaving for free brokers once they noticed ([joingerald, secondary](https://joingerald.com/learn/saving--investing/is-acorns-worth-it-2026)). Greenlight's cheapest plan costs about $72 a year ([Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/credit-cards/reviews/greenlight-debit-card/)). BusyKid layers on fees such as 50 cents per declined purchase and $5 for a replacement card ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/busykid-prepaid-card)). Qapital's move to paid plans drew a "disappointing" verdict ([The College Investor](https://thecollegeinvestor.com/20472/qapital-review/)).

**Number three is cancellation traps and surprise charges, now confirmed by regulators.** In March 2025 the FTC (the federal consumer-protection agency) reached a **$17M settlement with Cleo**. It had alleged that Cleo falsely promised "instant" cash, made cancelling hard, kept charging after repeated cancel requests, and told users they could not cancel until advances were repaid ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-reaches-17-million-settlement-with-cash-advance-company-cleo-ai)). Rocket Money users report bill-negotiation fees of 35–60% of first-year savings, charged without their knowing a negotiation was underway ([Wall Street Survivor](https://www.wallstreetsurvivor.com/is-rocket-money-worth-it/); [ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/finance/truebill.html)). Greenlight parents describe being billed monthly for long periods without receipts or clear emails ([ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/finance/online-banks/greenlight.html)). Stash users describe being told to relink a bank in order to cancel, then being charged immediately ([ConsumerAffairs Stash](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/robo-advisors/stash.html)).

**Number four is support that does not answer.** Step offers chat and email only, and users describe "canned responses" ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/us/ca/palo-alto/profile/financial-services/step-1216-1531244/complaints)). Robinhood users complain of "no correspondence with a human being" ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/robinhood.com)). In January 2025 the CFPB (the federal agency for consumer finance) ordered **Cash App's operator to pay $175M**, partly because for years it offered no live customer service agents. It must now provide 24-hour live-person support ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-orders-operator-of-cash-app-to-pay-175-million-and-fix-its-failures-on-fraud/)).

**Number five is "they changed the deal," and it matters most for Sprout's milestone bonuses.** Zogo reviewers say per-question rewards fell sharply. Cash-out thresholds climb (the first $5 gift card costs 5,000 points, then 10,000, then 15,000). Gift cards that once arrived within an hour now take weeks or sit "under review," and replaying lessons no longer earns points ([The Ways to Wealth](https://www.thewaystowealth.com/zogo-review/); [JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1474636588/zogo-finance-simplified/reviews)). The same resentment follows other changes. A long-time GoHenry user says that after Acorns bought the company, "there are monthly fees, glitches, and money gets lost in cyberspace" ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/gohenry-kids-debit-card-app)). Fidelity Youth dropped its 5-cents-per-swipe reward ([Google Play snippet](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.fidelity.teen&hl=en_US&gl=US)). Step's savings rate appears to have fallen from 5% to 3% ([BusinessWire 2023](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230517005139/en/Step-Launches-5.00-on-Savings-Balances-up-to-$250000); [Step Save page](https://step.com/save)).

Below these sit four further problems:

- **Bugs and broken bank links.** Rocket Money users describe a loop where the app keeps asking them to re-link a bank that is already linked ([unstar.app, lower reliability](https://unstar.app/blog/rocket-money-wont-link-bank-account-loop-reviews-2026)). BusyKid users hit repeated Plaid bank-link errors ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/busykid-prepaid-card)). Reddit parents called the Fidelity Youth app "half-baked" ([joingerald, second-hand](https://joingerald.com/learn/saving--investing/fidelity-youth-account-review-guide)).
- **Automatic savings that overdraw the user's bank account.** The CFPB fined Digit $2.7M in 2022 after about 70,000 overdraft complaints, despite its "no overdraft" promise ([CFPB consent order](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_hello-digit-llc_consent-order_2022-08.pdf)). Acorns settled an overdraft class action for $2.5M in 2021 ([SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1829797/000110465921084323/tm2116619-3_s4.htm)).
- **Game-like nudges toward risky trading.** Robinhood paid Massachusetts $7.5M and agreed to permanently drop confetti animations. The complaint also cited digital scratch tickets and push notifications aimed at young, inexperienced users ([WealthManagement](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/regulation-compliance/robinhood-pays-7-5m-to-settle-massachusetts-gamification-charge)).
- **Upsells inside "free" education.** The free version of Greenlight Level Up shows kids an ad asking them to get their parents to open a Greenlight account ([Fioney](https://fioney.com/greenlight-level-up-review/)).

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Money stuck: frozen or closed accounts, slow withdrawals, slow refunds | Step, Robinhood, Cash App, Greenlight, Acorns Early, BusyKid, Public, Stash |
| 2 | Flat monthly fees that eat small balances | Acorns, Greenlight, BusyKid, Stash, Qapital, Cleo |
| 3 | Hard cancellation and surprise charges | FTC v. Cleo ($17M), Rocket Money, Greenlight, Stash |
| 4 | No reachable human support | CFPB v. Cash App ($175M), Step, Robinhood |
| 5 | Rewards or terms cut after users are hooked | Zogo, Acorns Early (after GoHenry), Fidelity Youth, Step |
| 6 | Bugs and broken bank links | Rocket Money, BusyKid, Fidelity Youth, Acorns Early |
| 7 | Automatic transfers that cause overdrafts | CFPB v. Digit ($2.7M), Acorns class action |

## Requested features are mostly fixes for those failures

The notes found **no clean list of feature requests**. App-store developer replies, Reddit threads and teen-written reviews were all unreachable. So this ranking comes from complaints, from what users leave for, and from surveys, not from users typing "please add X." Read it as strong inference, not a tally.

The clearest request is **free or near-free use at small balances**. Users leave Acorns for zero-fee brokers once fees outweigh their returns ([joingerald, secondary](https://joingerald.com/learn/saving--investing/is-acorns-worth-it-2026)). A competitor's review notes that Acorns Early is a poor fit for families who want a free card ([Greenlight's review of Acorns Early, competitor source](https://greenlight.com/learning-center/reviews/acorns-early-review)).

Close behind is **real interest on savings**. In Cash App's survey, 77% of Gen Z say they would save more if they earned interest ([Cash App press](https://cash.app/press/savings-interest-for-teens)). Meanwhile, teen bank accounts such as Capital One MONEY pay 0.10% and Chase First pays nothing ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/banking/capital-one-bank/capital-one-money-teen-checking-account); [CNBC Select](https://www.cnbc.com/select/chase-first-banking-account-review/)).

**Understanding credit** is the third gap and is badly unmet. Surveys report that 58% of Gen Z want to understand how credit scores work and 42% want credit-building help ([Academy Bank](https://www.academybank.com/learn/media/in-the-news/academy-bank-survey-reveals-insights-into-gen-z-financial-habits-credit-knowledge-and-challenges)). 80% of teens have never heard of FICO scores or do not fully understand them ([JA USA](https://jausa.ja.org/news/press-releases/more-teens-are-participating-in-financial-literacy-courses-but-gaps-in-learning-evident-according-to-new-survey)).

Fourth is **an easy way out**: one-tap cancellation and simple withdrawal. The FTC's Cleo order now requires exactly this ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-reaches-17-million-settlement-with-cash-advance-company-cleo-ai)), and Acorns Early is criticized for having no simple way to move money back to a parent's bank ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/kids-banking/gohenry-kids-debit-card-app)).

Fifth is **teen autonomy and a clear path to independence**. In a Wells Fargo survey, nearly 70% of teens say they should be able to spend their own money without restrictions, compared with 44% of parents ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/better/lifestyle/teen-debit-cards-real-world-way-teach-your-kids-about-ncna1071256)). Reddit threads often ask whether an older teen should just get a normal bank account instead of a parent-controlled card ([LogicalDollar](https://logicaldollar.com/greenlight-review/)). Fidelity is reportedly folding its teen app into its main app, specifically to smooth the handoff at age 18 ([proverbs27flocks](https://proverbs27flocks.com/2026/04/27/fidelity-youth-account-changes-and-educating-teens/)).

Sixth is **trustworthy guidance where the decision actually happens**. Among investors under 35, 61% use social media for investment information. Those who follow finfluencers scored only 42% on a knowledge quiz, yet 63% rated their own knowledge as high ([FINRA Foundation](https://www.finrafoundation.org/sites/finrafoundation/files/2025-11/NFCS_Investor_Survey_Report_White_Paper.pdf)). Almost 1 in 5 Gen Z respondents report losing more than $1,000 to bad online advice ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/gen-z-says-tiktok-least-160235529.html)).

Two lower-ranked themes complete the picture. The first is **content pitched at the right level and tied to near-term life**. Teachers report that advanced EVERFI students "breezed through and became bored" while others struggled with vocabulary ([EVERFI blog](https://everfi.com/blog/k-12/the-importance-of-financial-literacy-and-challenges-around-implementation/)). Only 39% of teens are interested in learning to file taxes, against 70% of adults who think they should ([JA USA](https://jausa.ja.org/news/press-releases/more-teens-are-participating-in-financial-literacy-courses-but-gaps-in-learning-evident-according-to-new-survey)). NGPF, which teachers rate highly, uses games about gig driving, minimum-wage budgets and influencer income ([Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/next-gen-personal-finance)). The second is **automation with an overdraft guardrail**: a "don't move money if my checking account falls below $X" setting. That is a design inference from the Digit and Acorns cases, not a direct user quote.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Free, or no fee at small balances | Users leaving Acorns; complaints about Greenlight and BusyKid pricing |
| 2 | Real interest on savings | Cash App survey (77%); 0–0.10% on bank teen accounts |
| 3 | Plain-English credit education and credit building | Academy Bank (58% / 42%); JA USA (80% unfamiliar with FICO) |
| 4 | One-tap cancel and easy withdrawal | FTC Cleo order; Acorns Early, Greenlight complaints |

The table's real lesson is that **no incumbent combines free, teen-appropriate, credit-plus-saving-plus-investing education with real action**. Each fills one corner:

- Step: credit.
- Fidelity Youth and Schwab: investing.
- Greenlight and Acorns Early: parent control.
- Zogo: lessons.
- Qapital and Chime: automatic saving.

Each also charges a fee, sells an account, or both. The closest threat to Sprout is **Fidelity Youth**. ## What Sprout should build, avoid and say

### Build: Sprout's planned features ranked by how much evidence supports them

**Pay-yourself-first automation has the strongest evidence of any planned feature.** It is the same mechanism as Chime's 10% paycheck split and Qapital's rules, both credited with real behavior change. Make it the default in onboarding: when a student logs a paycheck or gift, Sprout suggests a percentage to set aside and remembers it.

**Named savings goals come second.** Give each goal a name, a target and a picture, so "Spring break" or "First car" feels real. Qapital's unlimited named goals are a core part of its appeal ([Money Crashers](https://www.moneycrashers.com/qapital-review/)).

**Credit tools are the most under-served need on the list.** Few youth apps teach credit, and surveys show strong appetite for it, so this is where Sprout can most clearly stand apart. Build a plain-English credit-score explainer, a "what happens to your score if…" simulator, and, for college students, a first-card checklist. Step proves young people respond to credit features, and Step charges nothing for them.

**Weekly check-ins should be a short, friendly reflection.** The model is Cleo's roast without Cleo's fees. When young adults are stressed about money, 69% check their balance and 64% make a budget ([Bank of America](https://newsroom.bankofamerica.com/content/newsroom/press-releases/2025/07/confronted-with-higher-living-costs--72--of-young-adults-take-ac.html)). So a 60-second weekly "how did you do against your plan" fits behavior that already exists.

**Lessons should be short and tied to what the student just did.** Unlock a credit lesson when the student opens their first card; unlock a diversification lesson when they look at their score. Let advanced students test out of basics, which fixes the EVERFI pacing complaint. Lean toward near-term topics (first paycheck, gig income, rent, first credit card) over retirement and tax forms.

### Build carefully: milestone bonuses and the investing tools

**Milestone bonuses are Sprout's biggest opportunity and its biggest risk.** Zogo shows that real money rewards draw people in, then turn into 1-star reviews when they shrink, slow down or get stuck "under review." The research suggests rewarding **real actions** (hitting a savings goal, keeping a weekly streak, finishing a pay-yourself-first month) rather than quiz answers. Quiz rewards invite click-through farming, the same thing that fuels a market for EVERFI answer keys ([Quizzma](https://quizzma.com/tag/everfi/)). Size bonuses so Sprout can afford them for years. Publish the rules. Never cut a bonus a student has already earned. Pay out quickly and visibly. A bonus that sits "pending" for weeks will produce exactly the stuck-money reviews this report ranks as the top complaint.

**Portfolio diversification scoring and the utility-function optimizer have the least direct evidence.** A "utility-function optimizer" is a tool that asks how much risk you can stomach and suggests a mix to match. No survey in the notes asks for either tool. The supporting signals are indirect: 37% of investors use fractional shares and 28% use automated investing ([Schwab](https://www.aboutschwab.com/schwab-modern-wealth-survey-2025)), and the finfluencer knowledge gap described above. Their real value is as the **opposite of gamified trading**. They reward a balanced, spread-out portfolio instead of frequent trades. Keep them simple. Present the optimizer as a "find your mix" quiz, not math. Frame both as education, not personalized investment advice. And test with real students whether they use these tools before investing heavily in them.

### Avoid

| Avoid | Why, from the evidence |
|---|---|
| Holding customer money or issuing a card in v1 | Every top complaint (frozen funds, slow withdrawals, refunds, disputes) comes from moving and holding money through partner banks. If milestone bonuses must land in savings, use a well-reviewed partner account the student already owns, and say plainly who holds it. |
| Any flat fee or "premium" tier on the core product | Fees on small balances are complaint #2 and the top reason users quit. Sprout's "free" is its sharpest weapon; one paywall undoes it. |
| Upsells, account-opening ads or partner pitches inside lessons | Criticized in Greenlight Level Up; it makes "free" feel like a funnel. |
| Rewarding quiz answers with cash | Invites farming, forces later cuts (the Zogo pattern), and does not change behavior. |
| Cutting earned rewards or changing terms quietly | "They changed the deal" anger follows Zogo, Acorns Early, Fidelity Youth and Step. |
| Automatic transfers without a balance guardrail | Digit's CFPB fine and Acorns' class action came from savings pulls that overdrew accounts. |
| Confetti, streak pressure or nudges toward options, crypto, margin or prediction markets | Massachusetts fined Robinhood over exactly these tactics aimed at young users. States are now moving on event contracts, and a New York bill would ban them for under-21s ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/betting/prediction-sites/guides/prediction-markets-timeline)). |
| Bot-only support | The CFPB now requires Cash App to offer 24-hour live support. Even a small team that answers within a day stands out. |
| A heavy parent-surveillance model for older teens | Teens want autonomy (70% vs 44% of parents); parent-control apps get outgrown. |

### Position

The evidence supports a positioning line close to **"Free forever. No fees, no catch, no stuck money — just a plan that grows with you from your first paycheck to your first credit card."** Each part answers a top complaint:

- "Free forever, no fees" answers fees and upsells.
- "No catch" answers reward cuts and surprise charges.
- "No stuck money" answers the frozen-funds anger.
- "Grows with you" answers the outgrow-it-at-18 gap, which Fidelity is spending real effort to fix.

Spell out three pledges in the app and on the website: **we will never charge you, we will never cut a bonus you earned, and you can leave in one tap.** Because the SEC withdrew its proposed rule on game-like trading features in June 2025 ([Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/alert/sec-withdraws-fourteen-rule-proposals)), there is no federal rule on this. That leaves room to say openly: **"We reward good habits, not trading."** Parents and schools will hear that as a safety promise.

One honest counter-argument belongs in any investor conversation. Zogo shows that free, reward-funded learning tends to depend on banks and credit unions paying for it ([Zogo blog](https://zogo.com/blog/turn-financial-education-into-real-roi)). When that money tightens, consumer rewards shrink. Sprout should decide early who pays for "free" and for the bonuses (schools, credit unions, employers or sponsors). Otherwise it risks repeating Zogo's arc.

## How much to trust these numbers

The researchers could not load the live pages for the Apple App Store, Google Play, Trustpilot, BBB, ConsumerAffairs, NerdWallet, FINRA, Fortune, Yahoo Finance or most review sites. **Almost every rating, review count and complaint summary above comes from search-engine snippets, review blogs or aggregators**, often with no visible review date. No figures were pulled from the CFPB complaint database, and no Reddit threads could be read directly; Reddit sentiment comes only through secondary summaries. Several sources are competitors with an interest in the result: Greenlight reviewing Acorns Early, Kikaroo, PennyTime, and BusyKid's own comparisons. Others are lower-reliability aggregators (unstar.app, checkthat.ai, joingerald.com, cashadvanceapps.com).

The notes specifically flag these figures as conflicting or unverified:

- **Robinhood's Trustpilot score** shows as 1.2/5 on some page versions and 3.9/5 on the main US page; this is unresolved.
- **Step's App Store rating** is reported as both 4.8 and 4.7.
- **Step's BBB rating:** a B- profile is confirmed, but an F-rated "Step" profile with a January 2026 BBB investigation note may or may not be the same company.
- **Cleo's "3.0 as of August 2026"** rating comes from one aggregator, and the platform it refers to is unclear.
- **Cleo's FTC refunds** being unpaid as of late September 2026 comes from a single secondary source.
- A reported **July 2026 46-state Cash App settlement** appears only on a low-reliability aggregator and may be confused with an earlier settlement.
- **Fidelity Youth:** whether the standalone app is being discontinued conflicts between sources, and so does whether its "Learn and Earn" rewards remain.
- The **date of Step's savings-rate cut** from 5% to 3% is unconfirmed.
- **Zogo's reward-cut figures** are undated, so it is unknown whether they describe 2023–2024 or 2025–2026, and its "paywall" claim is unconfirmed.
- **Acorns' 4.7 rating on 959K reviews** comes from Acorns' own page.
- **All company savings claims** (Qapital's $1,500–$4,300 a year, Digit's $1,800, Step's average 725 credit score) are unverified marketing figures.
- **30-day retention benchmarks** for finance apps conflict, ranging from about 11% to 38%.

One widely repeated statistic, that a "CFPB 2024 Financial Wellbeing Survey" found 67% of budgeting-app users found the apps unhelpful, **could not be traced to any primary source and has been left out of this report**. Finally, the survey numbers credited to Academy Bank and Forbes, and to JA USA and EVERFI, are mixed together in the snippets, so the exact attribution of individual percentages is uncertain.

None of these caveats change the ranking of themes. The same loves and hates appear across independent sources, apps and regulators. They do mean that **any specific star rating should be re-checked on the live page before it appears in a pitch deck**.

## Conclusion

The incumbents' biggest weakness is not missing features. It is broken trust at the moments that matter most: getting money out, cancelling, and being treated fairly when terms change. Their app-store ratings stay high because the everyday experience works, while their Trustpilot, BBB and regulator records show what happens when it doesn't. A free app that does not hold money avoids most of that risk from day one. Sprout's real exposure is narrower and fully in its control: whether milestone bonuses arrive quickly, stay funded and are never quietly cut.

The second insight is that "financial literacy" sells best when it is not presented as lessons. Families and young adults credit habits formed by doing: automatic saving, visible goals, a small nudge at the moment of spending, real credit and real investments. Sprout's lessons, scores and optimizer will earn their place only if each one leads to an action the student takes that week. If Sprout does that, while leaving out fees, upsells and trading nudges, it can tell parents, schools and students something no incumbent can say credibly: that it is built to make them better with money, not to make money from them.
